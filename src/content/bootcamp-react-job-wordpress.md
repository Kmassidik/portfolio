I finished the JavaScript immersive at Hacktiv8 ready to build everything in
React. Hooks, APIs, the whole modern stack. Then I got my first real job, sat
down on day one, and the task was WordPress. PHP. A thing the bootcamp never
mentioned once. And it was just me. No senior to ask, no team to lean on. Just me,
the admin panel, and Google.

### The gap nobody warns you about

Bootcamps teach the shiny stuff because that's what gets you hired. But a lot of
real companies quietly run on WordPress, and someone has to keep it alive. That
someone was me. There's a specific feeling when the thing you spent a year
learning is not the thing the job actually needs. You get over it fast, because
the site still has to work on Monday.

### What the job actually was

Day to day, it was keeping a WordPress world running:

![One WordPress install at the center, connected to the things set up around it: sites, subdomains, SMTP email, and per-user roles.](/blog/wordpress-setup.svg)

- **Keeping existing sites alive.** Updates, broken plugins, the things that
  quietly fall over.
- **Building new sites** from scratch.
- **Making templates** by grabbing a free theme off the internet and reworking it
  until it was what we actually needed. Not glamorous. Very effective.
- **Wiring up SMTP** so the sites could actually send email, which is its own
  small rabbit hole.
- **Setting up subdomains** for different sites and sections.
- **RBAC**, giving each user the right role and permissions so people could only
  touch what they were supposed to.

None of it was in the bootcamp. All of it I had to figure out live.

### Learning it alone

Being the only one meant every problem was mine. Nobody to tap on the shoulder.
So I did what you do: read the docs, searched the exact error, watched a video at
11pm, broke it, fixed it, broke it again. Slow, but it sticks. Learning something
cold with no one to ask is a skill of its own, and this is the job where I picked
it up.

### Was it exciting? No. Did it matter? Yes.

I'm not going to pretend WordPress maintenance was thrilling, because it wasn't.
But it was the start. It taught me to be resourceful, to not panic when I'm handed
something I've never seen, and to ship anyway. That turned out to matter more than
knowing React did.

This was where the real work started. It gets to data centers and Kubernetes
later.
